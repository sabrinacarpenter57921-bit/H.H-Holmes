Chapter five

Myth check

Twelve claims you have almost certainly heard, each traced back to where it actually came from. Two of them turn out to be true. One is true but for the wrong reasons. The rest are newspaper copy that outlived its newspapers.

0 Hold up
0 Partly true
0 Do not survive checking

✕  No documentary basis whatsoever

This is the number that appears on documentary posters and in encyclopaedia entries, and it has no source. It cannot be traced to any statement by Holmes, any police estimate, any coroner, or any court. It appears to have emerged from pulp true-crime writing in the twentieth century, where each retelling rounded the previous one upward.

The highest figure Holmes himself ever gave was twenty-seven, in a confession he was paid for, and several of those twenty-seven were subsequently found alive. The number this archive can defend is nine.

✕  Not supported by the records

He did advertise lodging and did pursue a hotel licence. But research through city licensing files, directories, insurance maps and newspaper advertising has failed to establish that the upper floors ever operated as a working hotel during the exposition, and not one missing fair visitor has ever been documented as having stayed there.1

The building was six miles from Jackson Park, in a residential district, and was still partly unfinished. The fair-hotel image is the load-bearing wall of the entire legend, and it is the part with the least underneath it.

◐  A real vault; an invented apparatus

The centralised control panel — a board of valves letting Holmes gas any room at will — was never produced in evidence, never photographed, and never testified to by any gasfitter or plumber who had worked on the building. It is a newspaper construction of August 1895.

What does appear to have existed is a single large airtight closet or vault adjoining his office, with a gas fitting serving it, and a bare footprint reported on the inside of the door. That is a far smaller claim than the legend, and considerably more disturbing.

✕  Misread objects

Cremation requires sustained temperatures around 800–1,000°C for hours. A domestic coal stove of the 1890s does not come close, and the object found in the basement is at least as consistent with the glass-bending kiln Holmes had been promoting to investors as with anything else.

The "acid vat" was reported as quicklime, which is not a body-disposal agent at all: it slows decomposition, which is exactly why it was used in mass graves to suppress odour. A lime pit in a building under five years of continuous plastering is unremarkable.

◐  The oddities were real; the purpose is contested

Windowless rooms, doors opening onto brick and a stair rising into a sealed ceiling are all attested by people who worked in and searched the building. Holmes really did hire and fire contractors constantly so that no single builder saw the whole job.

But there is a second, duller explanation that fits every fact: a man five years deep in debt, endlessly re-partitioning his own building to create new lettable rooms, dodging suppliers, and abandoning half-finished plans when the money ran out. The contractor churn is at least as well explained by not paying them as by concealment. Both readings remain live.

✕  She outlived him

Elizabeth S. Holton appears in later census returns and gave statements to the press after Holmes's arrest. The store changed hands by purchase, and the subsequent dispute was a commercial one over unpaid notes.

This is a useful test case: it is one of the very first things most accounts say about him, it has been repeated for over a century, and it is flatly false.

✕  No

Promoted in a 2017 television series and a book by a descendant, the theory requires Holmes to have been in Whitechapel during the autumn of 1888. The documentary record places him in the United States across that period — trading, marrying, appearing in city directories and pursuing business in Chicago.

The claimed handwriting match has not been accepted by document examiners, and the crimes have nothing in common in method, victim profile or motive: the Ripper killings were frenzied street attacks on strangers; Holmes killed people he knew, for money, indoors, and hid the bodies.

✕  Settled in 2017

The rumour was fed by the concrete-filled grave he requested. In 2017 the grave at Holy Cross Cemetery was opened under court authorisation at the request of his descendants. The coffin had to be cut free of solid cement.

The remains were decomposed, but the teeth and moustache survived, and dental comparison identified them as Holmes. He was hanged on 7 May 1896 and he is still in the ground in Delaware County.

✓  True — and worthless as evidence

He did write and sell that confession, in April 1896, reportedly for around $7,500. So the claim is literally accurate.

It is also the most misleading true statement in the case. He confessed multiple times in mutually contradictory versions; at least two named "victims" turned up alive; the dates conflict with verifiable records; and he withdrew the whole thing on the scaffold. Every subsequent inflated count in popular culture traces back to a document its own author repudiated.

✓  Documented in full

This is the part of the story people find hardest to believe and it is the best-evidenced thing he ever did. Alice and Nellie Pitezel were recovered from the cellar of 16 St. Vincent Street, Toronto, on 15 July 1895 by Detective Frank Geyer. Howard's remains were recovered from a chimney and stove in Irvington, Indiana, on 27 August 1895.

Geyer's book documents the search step by step, city by city, and remains the single most reliable source in the entire literature.2

✕  Survivorship bias with a list attached

The "Holmes curse" list circulates in a fairly fixed form: the trial judge, the jury foreman, the coroner, the priest, the arresting officer, all dead in strange circumstances. Checked individually, several of them lived long and entirely ordinary lives, and the deaths that did occur were unremarkable for men of that age in that era.

Frank Geyer — the person most deeply immersed in the case — retired comfortably into insurance investigation and died of natural causes in 1918.

◐  A marketing phrase, not a fact

The Bloody Benders of Kansas were killing travellers in the early 1870s. The Harpe brothers were killing along the frontier in the 1790s. Serial murder was not invented in Englewood in 1891, and the term "serial killer" itself did not enter common use until the 1970s.

What is defensible is narrower and more interesting: Holmes was arguably America's first mass-media serial killer — the first whose case was covered nationally, daily, by competing wire-served newspapers, and therefore the first to be turned into a product while the trial was still running. The distortion is not a later corruption of his story. It is his story.

✦

A caution about this page

Debunking has its own momentum, and it is possible to over-correct into implying that Holmes was merely a swindler who got a bad press. He was not. He chloroformed his own accomplice, then took that man's three children across two countries and killed all of them, and buried two girls under a rented cellar floor with a borrowed spade. The corrections on this page reduce the scale of his crimes. They do not soften them.
