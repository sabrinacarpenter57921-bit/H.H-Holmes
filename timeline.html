VI

Chapter six

Thirty-five years

From a farmhouse in Belknap County to a concrete grave outside Philadelphia — and then a hundred and thirty years in which the story kept growing without him.

SHOWING 32 ENTRIES

Origins

1861 – 1886
16 May 1861Gilmanton, N.H.

Herman Webster Mudgett is born

Third child of Levi Horton Mudgett and Theodate Page Price, in a strict Methodist farming household. The American Civil War is five weeks old.

4 July 1878Alton, N.H.

Marries Clara A. Lovering

He is seventeen. One son, Robert, follows. He never divorces her, and she learns what he became from newspapers in 1895.

1882 – June 1884Ann Arbor, Mich.

University of Michigan medical school

Graduates in medicine and surgery. Gains anatomical training, pharmaceutical knowledge, and a professional bearing he uses for the rest of his life. Later boasts — uncorroborated — of running cadaver insurance frauds while enrolled.

1885 – 1886New York / Philadelphia / Chicago

Becomes H. H. Holmes

Abandons Clara, drifts through a series of towns and short-lived jobs, and adopts the name Henry Howard Holmes. Arrives in Chicago.

Englewood

1886 – 1893
188663rd & Wallace, Chicago

Takes over the Holton drugstore

Employed by, then buying out, Elizabeth S. Holton. Contrary to the legend, she outlives him by decades; the ensuing dispute is about unpaid notes, not murder.

28 January 1887Minneapolis, Minn.

Marries Myrta Belknap — bigamously

Still married to Clara. A daughter, Lucy, is born; Myrta is installed in Wilmette and largely kept apart from the Englewood business.

1887 – 1892601–603 W. 63rd St.

The building goes up

Three storeys and a basement, built in stages on credit, with contractors hired and dismissed continuously and few of them ever paid in full. This is the structure the press will christen the Murder Castle eight years later.

December 1891Chicago

Julia and Pearl Conner disappear

Julia — his bookkeeper, his mistress, and reportedly pregnant — and her eight-year-old daughter are never seen again. No death records exist for either. No charge is ever brought.

Spring 1892Dwight → Chicago, Ill.

Emeline Cigrand is hired

Recruited from the Keeley Institute as a stenographer at a conspicuously generous salary. She writes home about an engagement to a wealthy Robert Phelps — a man who appears never to have existed.

December 1892Chicago

Emeline Cigrand disappears

The letters to Lafayette, Indiana, stop. Her family's enquiries lead nowhere for three years.

1 May – 30 Oct 1893Jackson Park, Chicago

The World's Columbian Exposition

Twenty-seven million admissions to a temporary white city six miles east of the building. Holmes advertises lodging; no record establishes that his upper floors ever operated as a hotel, and no fair visitor has ever been documented as a victim.

1893Chicago / Fort Worth, Tex.

Minnie and Anna Williams vanish

Minnie signs her Texas inheritance over to a Holmes alias, then disappears with her visiting sister. The Fort Worth property becomes the basis of the next scheme.

The Pitezel scheme

1894
17 January 1894Denver, Colo.

Marries Georgiana Yoke — bigamously, again

Under the alias Henry Mansfield Howard, while both earlier wives are living. She will travel with him through the Pitezel period and testify at his trial.

July 1894St. Louis, Mo.

Jailed for a drugstore swindle — and meets Hedgepeth

In the cell he describes the insurance plan to train robber Marion Hedgepeth and promises $500 for the introduction to a crooked lawyer. He never pays it. That unpaid debt destroys him.

2–4 September 18941316 Callowhill St., Philadelphia

Benjamin Pitezel is murdered

Chloroformed while insensible with drink, then staged as the victim of an exploded laboratory lamp. Ruled accidental. The $10,000 policy is claimed and paid.

October 1894Indianapolis → Toronto

The three children

Holmes takes Alice, Nellie and Howard Pitezel on a circuit of cities, moving them separately from their mother. Howard is killed near Indianapolis; Alice and Nellie in a rented Toronto cellar. Alice's letters home are collected by Holmes and never posted.

17 November 1894Boston, Mass.

Arrested

Taken on an outstanding Fort Worth horse-theft warrant after Hedgepeth's letter reaches the authorities and Fidelity Mutual's own investigators close in. Nobody is yet looking for the children.

The pursuit and the trial

1895
26 June 1895Philadelphia

Detective Frank Geyer sets out

With photographs of three children and a list of aliases, no jurisdiction outside Pennsylvania, and no expectation of success.

Late June – July 1895Cincinnati · Indianapolis · Detroit

The trail through the hotel registers

Geyer reconstructs the route from lodging books and rental agents, and establishes that in Detroit Holmes kept three separate parties — the girls, their mother, and his wife — within a few streets of one another.

15 July 189516 St. Vincent St., Toronto

Alice and Nellie are found

Buried three feet deep in the cellar of a house Holmes rented for a fortnight, using a spade borrowed from the neighbour and returned.

20 July – August 1895Chicago

The building is opened, and the legend is born

Police excavate the basement while competing newspapers invent the Murder Castle in daily instalments: gas chambers, a crematorium, a body chute, a hundred victims. No Chicago charge ever follows. This is the version that survives.

27 August 1895Irvington, Ind.

Howard is found

Charred remains, teeth and a scorched tin toy recovered from the chimney and stove of a rented cottage. Carrie Pitezel identifies the toy.

19 August 1895Chicago

Fire guts the upper floors

The building burns during the excavation frenzy — widely suspected at the time to be an insurance play arranged in advance, and never satisfactorily explained.

28 October – 2 November 1895Philadelphia

Trial and conviction

Commonwealth v. Mudgett, before Judge Michael Arnold: one count, the murder of Benjamin Pitezel. Holmes dismisses his counsel and defends himself for part of it. The jury convicts of murder in the first degree in under three hours.

The end

1896
April 1896Moyamensing Prison

Sells a confession to 27 murders

Reportedly for around $7,500. Within weeks, named victims are found alive and the dates are shown to conflict with the record. Nearly every inflated body count in circulation descends from this document.

7 May 1896Philadelphia

Hanged

Nine days before his thirty-fifth birthday. He retracts the confession on the scaffold, claiming only two deaths. The drop does not kill him cleanly; he is pronounced dead about twenty minutes later. Buried in concrete, at his own request, at Holy Cross Cemetery, Yeadon.

The afterlife

1896 – present
1896Philadelphia

Geyer publishes his account

The Holmes-Pitezel Case: A History of the Greatest Crime of the Century — a plain, procedural narrative of the search, and still the most reliable document in the literature.

1938Chicago

The building is demolished

After four decades as a local curiosity with shops still trading below. A United States Post Office is later built on the site, where it remains.

1940s – 1990sPrint

The count inflates

Pulp magazines and mass-market true-crime paperbacks recycle the 1895 coverage, each edition rounding upward, until "up to 200 victims" is repeated as established fact by sources that cite only each other.

2003New York

The Devil in the White City

Erik Larson's bestseller interleaves Holmes with the architects of the 1893 exposition. Carefully sourced from the surviving 1890s press record, it is narrative history rather than forensic audit — and it permanently welds Holmes to the fair in popular memory.

2017Yeadon, Pa. / Chicago

The grave is opened; the record is re-examined

Court-authorised exhumation at the request of his descendants confirms by dental comparison that the man in the concrete is Holmes, ending the substitution rumour. The same year, Adam Selzer's document-based reconstruction dismantles much of the Castle legend from city records.

TodayEverywhere

The legend keeps its lead

The corrected account is freely available and thoroughly documented. The gas chamber and the two hundred victims remain, by an enormous margin, the more popular version — which is, in the end, the most Holmes-like fact about the whole affair.
