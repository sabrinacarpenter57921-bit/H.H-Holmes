III

Chapter three

The register of the dead

Nine people. Not a number — nine people, most of whom knew him well and trusted him, four of them children. Where the record permits, they are described here as they lived.

On counting

Holmes confessed to twenty-seven murders and the legend claims two hundred. The confession was written for payment, lists people who were later found alive, and contradicts his own earlier statements. The two-hundred figure has never been traced to any source at all. This register lists only people whose existence and disappearance are independently documented and whose deaths are attributable to Holmes on evidence — a standard that yields nine names, and that will grow if better evidence ever emerges.

SHOWING 9 OF 9 RECORDS

Julia Smythe Conner c. 1860 – December 1891 · aged c. 31
Probable

A tall, striking woman from Davenport, Iowa, who came to Chicago when her husband Ned took a job at the jewellery counter in Holmes's building. She worked in the store, kept its books, and was capable enough that Holmes made her a manager. He also began an affair with her, in full view; Ned left in 1891. Julia stayed on with her daughter.

She was reportedly pregnant when she disappeared around Christmas 1891. Holmes later gave several accounts, including that she died during an abortion he performed. Neither she nor Pearl was ever seen again, and no death record exists for either.

Pearl Conner c. 1883 – December 1891 · aged c. 8
Probable

Julia's daughter, and the first child. She lived in the building with her mother and was, by the accounts of tenants, a familiar presence around the shops. She vanished at the same time as Julia and for the same reason: she was a witness.

In one version of his confession Holmes admitted killing them both. In another he denied it entirely. Pearl has no grave, no death certificate, and for most of the last century no place in the story except as a subordinate clause.

Emeline Cigrand c. 1868 – December 1892 · aged c. 24
Probable

A stenographer from Lafayette, Indiana, described by everyone who knew her as exceptionally attractive and exceptionally competent. She had been working at the Keeley Institute in Dwight, Illinois — the famous alcoholism-cure sanatorium — when Holmes recruited her to his office in the spring of 1892 at a conspicuously good salary.

She wrote to her family about an engagement to a wealthy man named Robert Phelps. In December 1892 the letters stopped. Her family's enquiries went nowhere, and it was only after 1895 that they learned who her employer had been.

Benjamin F. Pitezel 1857 – 2 September 1894 · aged 37
Convicted

A carpenter from Illinois and Holmes's accomplice for years — the man who fronted schemes, signed papers, and did the work. He drank heavily, had a wife, Carrie, and five children, and appears to have believed to the very last that he was a partner rather than a commodity.

The plan was that Pitezel would fake his own death and the family would collect $10,000. Instead Holmes killed him — chloroform, the post-mortem found, administered while he was insensible with drink — and staged the body in the back room of 1316 Callowhill Street, Philadelphia, as the victim of an exploded laboratory lamp. The insurance was paid out. This is the one murder for which Holmes stood trial, was convicted, and was hanged.

Alice Pitezel 1879 – c. 25 October 1894 · aged 15
Documented

The eldest Pitezel daughter, and the reason so much of this story can be reconstructed at all: Alice wrote letters. She wrote to her mother from Indianapolis, from Detroit, from Toronto — careful, homesick, dutiful letters describing the hotels and the weather and asking when they would all be together again.

Holmes collected the letters and never posted them. They were found among his effects and read aloud in court, and they remain the most devastating documents in the case. She and her sister were suffocated in the cellar of a rented house at 16 St. Vincent Street, Toronto, where Detective Geyer found them on 15 July 1895.

Nellie Pitezel 1883 – c. 25 October 1894 · aged 11
Documented

The middle child of the three taken. She had long braids, which Holmes cut off before the burial in an attempt to hinder identification — a detail Geyer recorded flatly and which did more than any rhetoric to fix the case in the public mind.

The two sisters were buried together in a shallow hole in the cellar of the Toronto house. Holmes had rented the house, borrowed a spade from the neighbour next door, and returned it.

Howard Pitezel 1886 – c. October 1894 · aged 8
Documented

The only boy, and the first of the three to be killed — separated from his sisters at Indianapolis, which is why Alice's later letters keep asking after him. Holmes found him difficult to manage and said so.

Geyer traced a cottage Holmes had rented in Irvington, on the eastern edge of Indianapolis, and on 27 August 1895 found charred remains, teeth and a piece of a toy in the chimney and stove. The boy's identity was confirmed in part by the toy, which his mother recognised.

Minnie R. Williams c. 1869 – c. 1893 · aged c. 24
Probable

A Texas heiress who came into a Fort Worth property inheritance and into Holmes's orbit around 1893. She worked in the building, was introduced as his wife, and signed the Texas property over to a Holmes alias — after which the Fort Worth land scheme with Pitezel became possible.

She disappeared in 1893. Holmes's story was that she had gone to Europe; later he claimed she had killed her own sister and fled, a version no one has ever corroborated. The property transfer supplies both the motive and the paper trail.

Anna "Nannie" Williams c. 1871 – c. July 1893 · aged c. 22
Probable

Minnie's younger sister, a schoolteacher from Midlothian, Texas, who came north to visit and to see the World's Columbian Exposition. She wrote home about the fair and about her sister's impressive fiancé.

Her letters stop in the summer of 1893. Both sisters vanish from the record at effectively the same moment, in the same city, in the same building, with the same man in possession of their property afterwards. She is the closest thing the case has to a genuine World's Fair victim — and even she was not a stranger passing through, but a guest who knew her host.

The ones who weren't

A register of victims is only honest if it also records who has been wrongly added to it. Several names carried for a century in books and documentaries do not survive checking.

Elizabeth S. Holton, the pharmacist's wife from whom Holmes took over the drugstore, is routinely listed as his first murder. She outlived him. Robert Phelps, the wealthy fiancé Emeline Cigrand wrote home about, appears never to have existed at all — the name was almost certainly one of Holmes's own inventions. Several of the twenty-seven people named in the 1896 confession read about their own deaths in the papers and wrote in to say they were fine.

And there is the darkest possibility in the other direction: Carrie Pitezel, Benjamin's widow, survived — but she spent months travelling the same circuit as Holmes, one or two cities behind her own children, believing she was being taken to meet them. She was in Detroit at the same time as her daughters, a few streets away, while Holmes moved between the two parties. Geyer's account of telling her what he had found in Toronto is the hardest passage in the literature.
