IV

Chapter four

Geyer's manhunt

Holmes was not caught by brilliant detection of his murders. He was caught because he cheated a train robber out of five hundred dollars, and then because one Philadelphia detective refused to accept that three children could simply stop existing.

The $10,000 policy

The plan Holmes described in a St. Louis jail cell in 1894 was simple and not original. Benjamin Pitezel would take out a life insurance policy for $10,000 with the Fidelity Mutual Life Association, set himself up in Philadelphia as a patent promoter under the name B. F. Perry, and then "die" in a laboratory accident. A substitute corpse would be produced. Pitezel's wife Carrie would identify it and collect. Holmes would take his share.

Marion Hedgepeth, the train robber sharing the wing, agreed to supply a crooked lawyer in exchange for $500. Holmes took the introduction and never paid.

On 4 September 1894 a body was found in the back room of 1316 Callowhill Street, burned about the face and chest, beside a broken bottle and a pipe. It was ruled accidental. The claim was filed. And no substitute corpse was ever involved: the body was Pitezel himself, chloroformed while insensibly drunk and then arranged.

The letter

From prison, Hedgepeth wrote to the authorities describing the scheme and naming Holmes. That letter reached the insurance company, which had already begun to have doubts, and the company put a detective on it. Holmes was arrested in Boston on 17 November 1894 — not for murder, but on an outstanding warrant for horse theft out of Fort Worth, Texas.

At that point everyone believed the case was an insurance fraud with one suspicious death. Nobody was looking for children.

The people who ended it

Marion Hedgepeth

Train robber. Wrote the letter that started everything, over an unpaid $500. Received a commutation for it, and was shot dead by a policeman in a Chicago saloon robbery in 1910.

W. E. Gary & Fidelity Mutual

The insurer that declined to quietly absorb a suspicious claim and instead paid for the investigation that exposed it.

Frank P. Geyer

Philadelphia detective, twenty years' service, recently bereaved of his own wife and daughter in a house fire. Given the file nobody expected to close.

Carrie Pitezel

Benjamin's widow. Handed three of her five children to Holmes and was moved around the country for months believing she was about to be reunited with them.

Summer 1895

Nine hundred boarding houses

Geyer left Philadelphia on 26 June 1895 with photographs of Alice, Nellie and Howard Pitezel and a bundle of Holmes's aliases. He had no forensic science, no national database and no jurisdiction outside Pennsylvania. He had train timetables, hotel registers, and the willingness to knock on every door in a city.

26 June 1895Cincinnati

The first trace

Working from hotel registers, Geyer establishes that Holmes and three children stayed in Cincinnati in late September 1894 under assorted names, and that Holmes rented a house on Poplar Street — into which he had a stove delivered, and then abruptly gave the house up. A neighbour had thought the delivery of a large stove for a man with no furniture peculiar enough to remember it.

Early July 1895Indianapolis

Howard separates from his sisters

Geyer traces the party to Indianapolis hotels, and finds that from a certain date onward only two children are recorded. Alice's undelivered letters, read alongside the registers, show her asking repeatedly after her brother. Geyer searches Indianapolis exhaustively and finds nothing. He moves on.

Mid July 1895Detroit

Three parties, one man

In Detroit, Geyer establishes the fact that defines the whole pursuit: Holmes had the two girls at one address, Carrie Pitezel and her remaining children at a second, and his wife Georgiana Yoke at a third — simultaneously, moving between them, keeping a mother within walking distance of daughters she was told she would see tomorrow.

15 July 1895Toronto

16 St. Vincent Street

Geyer and Toronto detective Alfred Cuddy work through the city's rental agents. They find a house Holmes rented in October 1894 for a fortnight. The neighbour recalls lending him a spade. In the cellar, under three feet of earth, Geyer finds Alice and Nellie Pitezel. Nellie's braids had been cut off and buried with them.

27 August 1895Irvington, Indiana

The cottage and the stove

Geyer returns to Indiana and starts again, this time on the outlying villages. In Irvington, a resident remembers a man who rented a cottage and asked repeatedly about the chimney. In the chimney and the stove Geyer finds charred bone, teeth, and a scorched tin toy that Carrie Pitezel identifies as Howard's.

July–August 1895Chicago

Meanwhile, the building

As Geyer's findings reach the papers, Chicago police open the 63rd Street building. Within a fortnight the excavation has become a national spectacle and the "Murder Castle" has been invented in print. No Chicago prosecution ever results — but this is the moment the legend outgrows the case, and it has never stopped growing since.

The trial

Commonwealth of Pennsylvania v. Herman W. Mudgett, alias H. H. Holmes opened in Philadelphia on 28 October 1895 before Judge Michael Arnold. The charge was the murder of Benjamin F. Pitezel — one count, one victim, no mention of Chicago.

The prosecution's case was medical and documentary: the post-mortem finding of chloroform in quantities inconsistent with an accident and with the position of the body; the staged scene; the insurance paperwork; and above all the identification evidence tying Holmes to every stage of the fraud.

Holmes dismissed his counsel on the second day and conducted his own defence for a period — cross-examining witnesses competently, by most accounts, and then allowing his lawyers to return. Georgiana Yoke, his third wife, testified. Carrie Pitezel testified. The verdict came on 2 November 1895 after deliberation reported at under three hours: guilty of murder in the first degree.

Appeals to the Pennsylvania Supreme Court and a clemency petition to the governor both failed.

The confessions

In April 1896, from his cell, Holmes sold a confession to the Hearst press — reportedly for around $7,500, a fortune at the time. In it he admitted twenty-seven murders and described his own body physically deforming into something devilish.

The document collapsed almost immediately. Named victims turned up alive. Dates contradicted verifiable records. He had already given at least two other, incompatible accounts. It remains the single most quoted source about him and the single least reliable, and virtually every inflated victim count in circulation descends from it.

What the conviction rests on

Chloroform found in the stomach in a quantity and distribution inconsistent with the staged accident; a body position inconsistent with the scene; the insurance application, correspondence and payout in Holmes's hand or under his direction; and the subsequent conduct — the removal of the children — as evidence of consciousness of guilt.

7 May 1896

Holmes was hanged in the yard of Moyamensing Prison, Philadelphia, on the morning of 7 May 1896, nine days before his thirty-fifth birthday. He was calm, spoke briefly, and used his last statement to retract the confession — saying he had killed only two women, by botched abortions, and no one else. It was the final version, and no more reliable than the others.

The drop was short and the fall did not break his neck cleanly. He was reported to have taken something over fifteen minutes to die, and was pronounced dead about twenty minutes after the trap opened.

He had left precise instructions for his burial, and they were followed: no embalming, a coffin filled with cement, the grave dug to twice the usual depth and topped with a further layer of concrete, in an unmarked plot at Holy Cross Cemetery in Yeadon, Delaware County. The usual explanation is that he feared grave-robbing by medical schools — the trade he had himself worked in.

The 2017 exhumation

The concrete grave fed a durable rumour that Holmes bribed his way out and sent another man to the gallows. In 2017, at the request of his descendants and with court authorisation, the grave was opened. The coffin had to be cut out of solid cement. The remains inside were badly decomposed but the teeth and the moustache were intact, and dental comparison identified them as Holmes.

He was hanged, he stayed buried, and he is still there.

Afterwards

A superstition grew that everyone connected with the case met a bad end — the warden, the priest, the jury foreman, the coroner, the arresting officer. The list circulates constantly and does not survive checking: several of the named men lived long, unremarkable lives, and Frank Geyer, the most exposed of them all, went on to a comfortable career in insurance investigation and died of natural causes in 1918.

The building burned, was patched, stood for forty more years, and came down in 1938. The legend, which needed no building, has done considerably better.
